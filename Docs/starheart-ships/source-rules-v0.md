### **Resting**

A short rest takes 1 day of travel and is called a “Refuel”. During refuel the ship makes 2 downtime moves. These replenish armor, stress, and hull points as they do with players. The final refuel move is to vault a ship domain card. The GM gains no fear on a refuel, but generally the party is taking a long rest around the same time.

“Refit” is a one week process in which the ship is fully repaired. Refit must be performed at a location that can handle the work. During a refit all hull, stress, and armor are cleared automatically. During a refit, if the physical elements of the domain card are available, a card in the ship's loadout or vault can be swapped for another card the ship is eligible for. Depending on the station engineers might need persuasion or a favor to do the work.

Potentially we could do something like cost here, but I’m not sure about that, since cost requires so much extra rigging. 

### **Character Domain Cards**

Domain cards for characters work on a personal level, but typically have no effect during space combat. The long distances, high speeds, and massive sizes involved mean the relevant cards would have an impact so diminished they should be ignored.

### **Ships Systems**

The components of a ship are broken into the following systems

**Transport** \- Engines make the ship go faster and make it harder to hit.

**Weapons \-** All the weapons, even the non domain weapons, are connected to the weapons system of the ship.

**Science \-** Finding points in the distance, acquiring target locks, and scanning landing zones are all done by sensors.

**Engineering \-** Miscellaneous tech that requires ship integration and power are captured here.

Domain cards are associated with these, as are ship hull points. Missing the parts of the hull protecting these systems means actions taken regarding them will have disadvantage. For example, missing the engines hull makes movement, escape, chase, etc more difficult. If a domain card is an Engine type, actions taken with it will be done with disadvantage.

### **Sensing and Sensor Range**

Some abilities refer to enemies you can sense. Sensing an enemy requires that they are within sensor range and that they are not obstructed by other ships or danger zones.

# Ships

A ship is a flying craft with a size that ranges from a missile (Tier 1\) to battle cruiser (Tier 5). Most PC personal ships are Tier 2, and the shared PC Hub Ship is Tier 3\. As a rough estimate:

| Size | Title | Occupants | Examples |
| :---: | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | Drone | 0 to 1 crew | Sputnik, F-15, Farscape-1 |
| 2 | Flier | 1 to 2 crew | TNG Shuttlecraft, Defiant |
| 3 | Catamaran | 2 to 6 crew up to 20 passengers | Serenity, Moya, Millennium Falcon |
| 4 | Cruiser | 5 to 100 crew Up to 300 passengers | TNG Enterprise, Klingon Bird of Prey |
| 5 | Dreadnaught | 100+ crew Up to 10000 passengers | Star Destroyer, Borg Cube |

### **PC Ship Statistics**

A ship is described by 4 systems \- Engineering, Transport, Weapons, and Science. Making rolls associated with these systems will be referred to as making a Transport roll, Weapons roll, etc. 

PC ships have hull points for each system. They have 1 plus, plus 1 if they have any domain cards for that system, plus any added by those domain cards. Actions for the ship are taken using that system. Piloting, escaping, and maneuvering is typically a Transport action. Firing and boarding systems, weapons. Analysis, targeting, comms, science. Gadgets and tricks, engineering. If the hull points for a system are marked, the actions are done with disadvantage.

PC Ships have a trait for use in each system. All ships use Knowledge for Science, but the other systems can vary depending on what kind of ship they are. A quick fighter craft might use agility for Transport rolls, while a bulkier rust bucket might require strength to operate massive levers.

PC ships have 6 Stress, plus any granted by domain cards. Stress is used as a representation of taxing the ship’s systems to get results or sustain damage.

PC ships have thresholds depending on their model (change later?). When marking HP on a ship the PC picks which category to mark unless the enemy was targeting a specific category with a feature.

When all HP are marked, the PC ship is adrift, meaning the people on board are alive but at the mercy of other ships nearby until they can restore the ship to operation.

If the PC ship continues to take damage, it marks stress for each HP it would have marked. When the final stress is marked, the PC and Ship both make a death move, but they must be different death moves.

### **Adversary Ships**

Adversary ships have all the same statistics that a regular adversary has, with a couple exceptions.

All adversary ships have a piloting bonus and a weapon bonus. The GM can elect to add one of these bonuses to the ship’s difficulty without spending a fear.

Enemy ships have HP and stress. For each marked HP the enemy ship suffers \-1 to rolls, to a lower penalty of \-6. Enemy ships follow the same rules concerning HP and stress: marking all HP doesn’t destroy the ship but renders it dead in the water. Marking all stress kills occupants and destroys the integrity of the ship, rendering any plunder inside ruined without special protections.

If a specific adversary pilots a ship and they have an experience that is higher than the ship’s piloting or weapons, it is added instead of the ship’s bonus, again without needing to spend fear. Use the ship’s difficulty for ship interactions, not the pilots. 

Thus firing at a difficulty 10 ship with a difficulty 18 pilot requires rolling a 10 to attack. If the ship has a \+2 Evasion bonus, the attack against it requires a 12\. If the pilot has the experience “Slippery Bastard \+3”, the GM can rule this supersedes the \+2 Evasion, and the difficulty to hit the ship is now 13\.

### **Docking**

Docking is the act of connecting one ship to another whereby individuals or goods can be transferred between them. Docking between willing ships is automatic under most circumstances, but might require a piloting roll under duress. Forced Docking, where one ship approaches to dock without permission, is very difficult against an unwilling ship, but less so against a ship with all hull points marked (rendering it disabled.)

To attempt a docking maneuver against another ship, make a Piloting check with disadvantage against the enemy’s difficulty \+ Piloting bonus. As with other shipborne difficulties, the GM may instead apply an experience from the pilot if one suits this.

To attempt a docking maneuver against a disabled ship, make a Piloting check against the ships difficulty with no bonuses. While disabled, if the pilot has a specific experience that would help the GM must spend fear to add it.

When an adversary ship tries to board a PCs ship, the PC can make a Transport reaction roll with advantage to avoid it. If the PC ship’s Transport systems are fully marked, the roll does not have advantage (per the normal rules of applying disadvantage to related rolls).

On a failure, the aggressor must mark a hull point.

On a success, the both ships gain the *Docked* condition. All ships that are touching and have the Docked condition are considered to be docked with each other.

While *Docked* to a ship of the same or larger size, the docked ship cannot make Transport action rolls.

While *Docked*, if a ship would mark more than 1 hull point, it can spread one of those hull points to another docked ship if it so chooses.

For example, if Ship A is docked with Ship B, and for any reason Ship A must mark 2 hull points, Ship A can instead elect to mark 1 Hull point and force ship B to mark the other. If Ship A had to mark 3 hull points instead, they could give 1 to ship B, but would have to mark 2 themselves. And if they only had to mark 1 hull point, they can’t elect to spread it to ship B.

### **Boarding**

Without special domain cards, boarding requires being Docked. Boarding to a willing ship requires no checks: creatures move through the connected hatch between ships with ease.

Without permission, boarding is often harder. The specific situation can be designed by the GM, but will likely include elements like armed defenders, anti intruder measures, smoke filled hallways, locked doors, and rooms opened to space, whether as part of the battle or part of the ships defenses.

While boarding ships are usually close enough for PC abilities to be effective. Firing guns or spells down ship hallways or inspiring fear in enemies through closed hatch windows are all back on the table.

### **Undocking**

Removing the docked condition between two willing ships is usually a standard procedure with no roll required. Environmental circumstances may warrant a roll to safely undock.

A ship that is disabled (all Hull Points are marked) cannot undock, and cannot prevent another ship from undocking.

If a PC ship is attempting to undock from a forced docking situation, the ship’s pilot makes a Transport Action roll with disadvantage against the other ship. On a success with Hope, the ships are undocked. On a success with Fear, the player chooses: detach and mark 1 Hull Point or remain attached.

If an adversary ship is attempting to undock, the PC ship gets a Transport Reaction roll with advantage. On a failure, the enemy ship can detach, but if the Hope die is higher than the Fear die, the enemy must choose to mark 1 hull point in order to do so.

### **Attacking**

An attack is any action taken with the intention of causing damage. Weapons attached to a ship have traits associated with them just like character weapons do. Use that trait for the action roll to attack with them.

If all weapon hull points have been marked, attacks are made with disadvantage.

Some domain cards are weapon system cards. They will either list the trait needed or specify a Piloting roll, in which case either piloting trait can be used for the ship.

The phrase “**With Lock:”** indicates that the effects require that the target be Locked.

***Locked*** can be applied by making a Science check against an enemy difficulty. *Locked* grants no inherent benefit apart from what equipment or domain cards specify it does. *Locked* is lost the party acquires a new Locked target, or if the Locked target cannot be sensed by any PC ship.

Any ship can attempt to lose the Locked condition with an action. The PC closest to an enemy taking such an action makes a Piloting Sensors reaction roll against the difficulty of the enemy. On a failure, the Locked condition is removed. A PC trying to lose the Locked condition makes a Piloting Engines roll against the highest difficulty of the enemies that can see them. On a success, the Locked condition is removed, and on a critical success the player may elect to apply the Locked condition to an enemy they can sense.

### **Damage**

Ship thresholds do not vary by tier to the extent that characters do. Instead, a larger ship subtracts its tier difference from the hull points it would normally mark. A Tier 2 ship doing 3 HP of damage to a Tier 4 ship ends up doing only 1 damage. Tier 4 \- Tier 2 is 2, and 3 \- 2 is 1\.

For PCs, hull damage is assigned to a system. Marking all hull slots for a system means that system has disadvantage. Marking all hull points means the ship is non functional and cannot be operated. This includes making attacks or flying.

Unlike player characters, marking the last Hull point does not destroy the ship. Further damage is marked on the Stress slots instead. Once every box has been marked the player characters inside should make a death move. The ships pilot and the ship each make their own death moves, and the moves must be distinct.

For adversaries, each marked hull point incurs a \-1 penalty, to a maximum of \-6, on action and reaction rolls for that enemy.

## **Ship Maelstroms**

When the action is happening around a focal area, like a capital ship, station, asteroid, or warp nexus, the ships are participating in a Maelstrom.

In a Maelstrom ships are assumed to be zipping around in loops, taking tight corners, and trying to give as well as they get, similar to how we might imagine combat between player characters and adversaries. Use the same rules for distance: melee, very close, close, far, and very far, but extend these distances to roughly kilometers instead of feet. Melee is adjacent to 5km. Very Close starts there and extends out to 15 km. Close ends at 30km, Far at 100km, and Very Far at 300km. These are approximate numbers meant to emphasize the scale of space combat.

During a Maelstrom, player character abilities are typically not useful. There are exceptions, like Teleport, but ships should not be considered “creatures”, even if they are living ships, and during a Maelstrom ships rarely come within the personal definition of Very Far range of each other, so many targeting spells won’t work.

## **Ship Chases**

During a Chase, all ships are assumed to be moving in the same direction towards the same target. Once any ship in a scene attempts to leave the scene all other ships that can fly can elect to initiate a chase. 

During a chase scene the rest of the world is moving around the ships, but the ships are kept in order unless something interrupts that order. 

### **Starting a Chase**

To start a chase scene, designate a lead ship, usually the one that was leaving the scene. Put the lead ship roughly in the middle of the table, with room for 3 rows of ships behind and and 2 or 3 in front of them.

**If the lead ship is an NPC ship**, there may be one or two other NPC ships in the same band, but the rest should be distributed in the next 3 bands behind it in an order that makes sense for the scene and the likely reaction times of the ship. There’s no need to roll for these positions.

Players should make reaction rolls. If they beat adversary Transport Difficulty in the scene, put them in the band directly behind the lead ship. If they beat some of the ships, put them in the next band back. If none, put them in the third band. After this placement, any PCs can move backward as many bands as they’d like.

**If the lead ship was a PC ship**, have that PC make a Transport action roll. Enemies whose Transport Difficulty is higher than the result can start in any range band behind the lead ship. All others must start 2 bands back. On a roll with Hope other PCs can be in any of the range bands behind them, and can place their ships after the enemies have been placed. On a roll with Fear the other PCs will make reaction rolls as described above and place accordingly before the enemies are placed.

### **During a Chase**

The spotlight passes normally just as it would during any other Daggerheart scene or Maelstrom. 

Chases use bands. Each band contains ships that are beside each other. To figure the distance between two ships in the same band, use whatever method you were using previously for Melee, Very Close, Close, Far, and Very Far.

Some ship domain features are marked with the key phrase “**Forward Facing**”. These features only provide benefits against a target in a band in front of you, not the same band or one behind you.

Other domain features are marked with the key phrase “**Aft Facing**”. As you might expect, they are only usable on a target behind your current band.

Changing bands once uses your movement. Trying to change bands twice requires a Transport Action roll. If any adversary in the chase is smaller than you, roll with disadvantage.

If you beat all adversaries in the chase, you can move two bands. If you beat some adversaries, you move one band and one adversary you didn’t beat can move one band as a reaction.

The band distance (Close, Far, Very Far) is the **smallest distance** the ship could be away from you, but could be further depending on its distance to the left or right of you.

When an enemy tries to maneuver multiple bands, the closest PC ship makes a Transport Reaction roll against them. On a success, the enemy moves one band and that PC may move one band. On a failure, the enemy moves two bands.

#### **Stationary Objects**

During a chase the ships are assumed to be moving very quickly through space. Stationary objects, like debris or an asteroid, pass through the whole stack at once in a column. The GM determines the width of that column, but all ships in it are affected, so if an environment calls for a reaction roll to get out of the way, all ships, adversary and PC, make the same check to get out of the way.

#### **Leaving a Chase**

There are a couple of ways a ship could leave a chase.

First, a ship can drop out voluntarily. An NPC or PC can announce this intent. If no NPC wishes to pursue them, a PC becomes an Observer. If an NPC wishes to pursue them, the PC needs to make a Transport roll against the most difficult pursuer. On a success, they become an OBserver. If a PC wishes to keep an NPC in a chase, the PC makes a Transport reaction roll against them. Only one PC does so. On a failure, the NPC becomes an Observer.

When a participant fails to leave a Chase, they instead move forward or backward one range band, and the person pursuing them makes the same movement (forward or backward one range band).

Another way by outpacing all other participants. Allied or solo Ships reaching Very Far range of all other participants are Observing the chase. If an Observer chooses to act in the scene again, they put their ship back into the stack in whatever band they’d like and on the far left or far right side at the start of their spotlight.

Observers are generally considered safe from the environmental effects of a chase, with exceptions as the GM sees fit, as the Observers can devote their full attention to careful flying while Observing.
